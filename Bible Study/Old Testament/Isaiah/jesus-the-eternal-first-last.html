ALPHA AND OMEGA, BEGINNING AND END, FIRST AND LAST

The book of Revelation also presents Jesus as making divine “I am” self-declarations. It uses three titles that mean the same thing: “the Alpha and the Omega” (to alpha kai to ō), referring to the first and last letters of the Greek alphabet; “the First and the Last”; and “the Beginning and the End.” None of these titles occurs elsewhere in the New Testament. The title “the First and the Last” clearly originates at least in part from Isaiah, in which the Lord insists that he is the only God: “I, the Lord, the first, and with the last; I am he.... I am the first and I am the last; besides me there is no god.... I am he; I am the first, and I am the last” (Isa. 41:4; 44:6; 48:12). In the context of Isaiah’s prophecies, the Lord is asserting in these statements that he is the one in control of Israel’s future and that God’s people have a sure hope of restoration.

Beyond controversy, Revelation applies the title “the First and the Last” to Jesus at least twice: “Don’t be afraid. I am the First and the Last, and the Living One. I was dead, but look—I am alive forever and ever, and I hold the keys of death and Hades” (1:17b–18 CSB). “Thus says the First and the Last, the one who was dead and came to life” (2:8 CSB). Jesus asserts that by his death and resurrection he has conquered death, and he is assuring his people of their future resurrection and vindication in the age to come. Thus, the title “the First and the Last” as applied to Christ has a theological significance in Revelation that is parallel to its application to Yahweh in Isaiah.

Jehovah’s Witnesses deny that Revelation also applies the titles “the Beginning and the End” and “the Alpha and the Omega” to Jesus. They point out that these titles apply explicitly to God (Rev. 1:8; 21:6). Near the end of the book, however, both titles appear alongside the title “the First and the Last,” which we know belongs to Jesus:

The speaker’s statement, “I am coming soon” (erchomai tachu), is the second of three such declarations in the closing lines of the book (Rev. 22:7, 12, 20). Jesus is very clearly the person speaking in the third of these statements: “He who testifies to these things says, ‘Surely I am coming soon.’ Amen. Come, Lord Jesus!” (22:20). Jesus made the same declaration at least twice earlier in the book, quoted with the same Greek words (2:16; 3:11). No one else in Revelation says he is “coming quickly,” and this is not said about anyone else, including the Father. Stafford attempts to rebut this point by noting that in Revelation 11:14 the expression is not used of Jesus’ coming.51 However, there it is not a person but “the third woe” that John says “is soon to come” (erchetai tachu).

In the same context as one of his declarations that he is coming soon (Rev. 2:16), Jesus also warns that he is the one who will repay people according to what they have done (2:23). Here again, this role is attributed in Revelation only to Christ. Thus, the internal evidence of the book as a whole, as well as the immediate context in verse 20, strongly supports the view that the speaker in Revelation 22:12 is Jesus.

What, though, about Revelation 22:13? There certainly is no indication that the speaker changes between Revelation 22:12 and 22:13.52 The book has already called Jesus “the First and the Last” twice (1:17; 2:8). It has also called Jesus “the Beginning of the creation of God” (3:14 NKJV), using the same word for “Beginning” (archē) as in the expression “the Beginning and the End” in Revelation 22:13. In view of these contextual elements, and the fact that “the Alpha and the Omega” and “the Beginning and the End” are undoubtedly synonymous with “the First and the Last,” we conclude along with a near consensus of scholars that Revelation 22:13 does assign these titles to Jesus.53 This text, then, provides an extremely strong affirmation of the deity of Christ. As Richard Bauckham comments, “As a way of stating unambiguously that Jesus Christ belongs to the fullness of the eternal being of God, this surpasses anything in the New Testament.”54

THE REVELATION OF THE NAMES OF JESUS

The book of Revelation assigns other divine names or titles to Jesus. For example, it refers to Jesus as the “husband” or bridegroom of the people of God (Rev. 19:7–9; 21:2, 9), a motif found elsewhere in the New Testament (e.g., Mark 2:19; 2 Cor. 11:2; Eph. 5:23–27). This idea derives from the Old Testament motif of Yahweh as the husband of Israel (e.g., Isa. 54:4–8; Jer. 31:31–32; Hos. 2:1–3:5).55 Revelation also calls Jesus “Lord of lords and King of kings” (Rev. 17:14) and the reverse form, “King of kings and Lord of lords” (19:16). Here again, the title derives from Old Testament descriptions of Yahweh (Dan. 4:37 LXX; cf. Deut. 10:17; Ps. 136:2–3).56

The very wealth of divine titles with which the New Testament writers shower the Lord Jesus Christ shows that they are serious when they refer to Jesus as Lord and God. The Gospels report that Jesus was careful during his earthly ministry to speak of his divine identity humbly and indirectly, and yet at times his self-declarations were recognized by his contemporaries as implicit claims to deity (e.g., John 8:58–59). The New Testament writers are themselves less reserved: they call Jesus Christ “God,” not once, but several times (John 1:1, 18; 20:28; Rom. 9:5; Titus 2:13; Heb. 1:8; 2 Peter 1:1; 1 John 5:20), and affirm numerous times that Jesus is “Lord,” doing so in contexts that equate Jesus with YHWH, the God of the Old Testament (e.g., Rom. 10:9–13; 1 Cor. 8:6; Phil. 2:9–11; 1 Peter 2:3; 3:15).

The New Testament gives Jesus all these names in the broader setting of a pervasive attitude of exalting the name of Jesus above every other name (Eph. 1:21; Phil. 2:9–11). Thus, if we are to be faithful to the teaching of the Bible, we must acknowledge Jesus Christ as our great God and Savior.
