8 Alcinous also considered that the first God was ‘above the heavens’ (Handbook 28.3), and incapable of local motion (Handbook 10.7). Numenius has the first god remaining idle in the creation, while the creator god moves through the heaven (fr. 12.13–14)...

Contrary to Minns’ skepticism, Justin is clear in what he believes about Christ. But first, let me highlight some of Minn’s key admissions regarding Justin’s Christological/Theological views:

Christ is the rational/reasonable Power of God who was begotten from/out of God’s own essence from before all creatures. Contrary to Minns, this clearly shows that Justin did not think the Logos is a creature or is among the things which God created.

Other scholars understand Justin to be affirming that God and his Word are equal in substance. This shows that Minns isn’t the last word on the subject, or the infallible authority on Justin’s Christological views.   

The transcendent God who loves his creatures sent forth his begotten Word (and the angels as well) in order to directly involve himself with his creation.

Related to this fact, the uncreated Word was sent into the world to become incarnate, i.e., to become the Man Christ Jesus.

The prophets were inspired by the DIVINE Spirit.

Plato’s phrase “the third about the third” is taken by Justin to be a reference to the Holy Spirit, which points to the Trinitarian shape of his theology. After all, the only way Justin could see in this expression to three an allusion to the Holy Spirit is if he had the three Divine Persons of the Godhead in view.

This may explain why elsewhere, this same writer Minns can speak of the Trinitarian understanding of Justin’s writing:

With the foregoing in view, I will now demonstrate that the scholars like Osborn who argued that Justin taught that the Word shares the same substance of God are correct, and that Minns is wrong. I will establish this by quoting the places where the blessed saint identifies Jesus as the YHWH God Almighty spoken of and worshiped throughout the Psalms.  

I will also prove that Justin did think along the lines of Trinitarian beliefs since he speaks of Christians as being those who worship three Persons, namely God, Christ and the prophetic Spirit.

Here are the Psalms, which Justin believed were all about Christ:

Since these Psalms are all about YHWH God, this means that Justin is arguing for Jesus being the human incarnation of YHWH God Almighty. And since both the Psalms and the rest of the prophet scriptures explicitly teach that YHWH is uncreated and the only true God,

This means that Justin must have believed that Jesus Christ is the only true God, and therefore coequal to the ineffable and nameless God who begot him. There’s simply no way around this fact. Therefore, Minns is simply mistaken in being uncertain concerning Justin’s view of Christ being equal to the Father in substance.

2 Literally: 'and the other good angels.' Two difficulties arise from this passage. (I) Justin seems to imply that Christ, too, is one of the angels; but 'other' (allos) is in Greek often used pleonastically for the second of two separate subjects, meaning no more than 'in addition: Cf. also French now autres citoyens, 'we citizens: (2) The passage would commit Justin to the worship of angels.
